Archival Intelligence Project

Handwriting Collator: an instrument that compares hands across archives and finds probable matches.

First results · November 2026

Mission

The Archival Intelligence Project is building an instrument to recover lost networks of social and intellectual exchange in the Renaissance, by layering pattern recognition in the manuscript record over transcribed texts and metadata.

The idea took shape on a research trip to the British and Bodleian Libraries in 2024, working with the marginalia that early modern readers left as they used books to work through real problems: idiosyncratic shorthand commentaries, coded lists, and astonishing sketches among them. The means of working out who made such marks, let alone what else the same person wrote in other volumes, remain strikingly underdeveloped — still a matter of paging through incomplete indexes and partial accounts of personal libraries.

Two decades of digitization have made a vast body of early print searchable, yet a scholar who knows the material inside and out is still more likely to find the striking connection than any query. Manuscripts and marginalia are crucial for establishing ownership, authorship and intellectual history, and they cannot be transcribed by hand at scale. New capacities in visual pattern recognition change what is possible, provided the work is grounded in visual analysis as such and not only in visual analysis for the purpose of transcription.

The project’s aim is a maximally dense reconstruction of largely lost social networks in intellectual history. What that promises is more than information about who wrote to whom, or where a missing text might be found. We hope it will yield a topologically coherent understanding of how new meaning emerges from networks of exchange, so that the social incubators behind every historical advance in human thought can be understood, and new means found of fostering such incubators in the present.

The instrument

The first instrument is a collator for handwriting, in the tradition of the Hinman Collator: it compares hands across collections at a scale no reader can.

It searches a large archive of page images and returns candidate matches for the page in question, each with a stated probability that the same person wrote both.

The judgment stays with the scholar. Its output is a lead at a stated probability, with the reasons recorded, for a scholar to evaluate.

Two test cases

Marginalia

Give the instrument one annotated page and ask whether the annotator’s hand appears anywhere else. This is where the project began, in 2024.

The missing manuscript

Give it pages in a known hand and ask it to find writing by that person which is not known to be theirs.

The first two figures involve well-known writers; the third tests the harder case of hands compared across different documents.

The archive

Behind the instrument is a graded archive of manuscript images, built to outlast any particular model.

2,456page images
376items
274writers
9source collections

Every attribution carries a grade, from autograph and documented to conjectural, and every judgment the instrument makes is stored as a claim with its probability and source. Original images are never altered.

Partners

The project depends on the libraries that hold the manuscripts and on the scholars who know the hands.

Initial work was conducted at the Bodleian and Folger Shakespeare Libraries. Discussions for formal partnerships are in progress.
